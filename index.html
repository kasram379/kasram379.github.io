Research

The thesis, in plain language

Mitigating Electricity Grid Congestion with Medium-Temperature Aquifer Thermal Energy Storage — A Technical Feasibility Analysis

Rotterdam wants to turn its old harbour docks into a neighbourhood. The heating has to be electric. The problem is that the grid there is already at capacity, and heat pumps pull hardest on exactly the evening the network has least to give.

So I asked a narrower question: if you store heat in the aquifer beneath the site at a higher temperature than conventional ATES, can you shrink the contracted peak — not the annual energy, the peak — enough to matter?

The honest answer is a qualified one, and it's the part I find most interesting. Standalone, medium-temperature storage costs you: annual electricity, money, emissions all go up. The gain isn't reduction, it's redistribution — moving demand off the hour the grid cares about. That trade only pays for itself in mixed deployments, where part of the district runs on storage and part doesn't.

Toolkit

What I actually use

Simulation

TESPy for thermodynamic cycles and heat pump performance. SEAWAT / MODFLOW for variable-density groundwater and heat transport. Coupling the two, which is where most of the real work hides.

Data & code

Python — NumPy, pandas, matplotlib — for everything from load-profile pipelines to post-processing a few hundred simulation runs. A working reimplementation of the BDEW standard load profiles, and the ALPG household generator wrangled into something usable.

Energy systems

Building and district demand modelling, ATES and heat pump system design, and the Dutch regulatory side: ACM tariff structures, congestion contracts, DSO flexibility products.

Writing it down

LaTeX and TikZ, to the point of having opinions about float placement. I care about figures that explain something rather than decorate a page.
